*Kathakar, 25 September 2026, Wave 4. This is the readable copy: the English
spread, the Marathi retelling, and the notes a native speaker and the owner

Wave 7 grows this into a whole short storybook. This spread is written as

## English

> The sky turned the colour of an old kadai, all at once, and Aaji called,
> "Quick, the papad!" Ira ran out to the courtyard, laterite hot under her
> bare feet, and caught the first drop on her palm before it could catch
> her. Then another. Then the whole sky let go.
>
> The stone went dark red, drop by drop, faster than Ira could count.
> Somewhere near the well, a frog answered the thunder. Aaji tucked the
> papad basket under her arm, laughing, rain already in her hair.
>
> Ira didn't run inside. Not yet. She wanted to smell the earth wake up.

101 words. Two pages: page one runs from "The sky turned" to "the whole sky
let go" (the drop that starts it); page two runs from "The stone went dark
red" to the end (what the rain does, and Ira staying out in it).

## For the native reviewer

- **जांभा दगड** (*jambha dagad*, laterite stone) — I believe this is the
  ordinary Marathi/Konkani term used in Goa for the red laterite the region
  is built from, and that a Goan family would recognise it without
  explanation. I have not heard it used in a children's story register
  specifically; please confirm it reads naturally aloud to a five-to-eight
  year old, or suggest the word a Goan grandmother would actually use.
- **काखेत धरली** (held under the arm/armpit) — chosen for "tucked under her
  arm" because it is a common, warm, physical idiom in Marathi. Please
  confirm it doesn't read as odd or overly literal for a basket.
- **हाक मारली** vs a plainer "म्हणाली" — I chose "hāk māralī" (called out)
  over "shouted" (ओरडली, which felt too sharp for a grandmother's warmth)
  and over "said" (which felt too flat for the urgency of "quick, the
  papad!"). Please confirm the register is right — affectionate urgency,
  not alarm.
- **कोसळलं** for "let go" (of the sky) — a common word for rain pouring
  down; please confirm it doesn't read as violent or frightening for this
  age group.
- The retelling keeps the same three-paragraph, three-beat shape as the
  English (the sky turns and the first drop; the downpour and Aaji's
  laugh; Ira staying out to smell the earth) rather than translating
  sentence by sentence, per Kathakar's brief. Please confirm the Marathi
  paragraph breaks land in the right places for reading aloud.
- This is a first draft only (kernel axiom A5): machine translation was not
  used, but I am not a native speaker, so nothing here should ship until a
  native speaker has read it aloud to a child or imagined doing so.

## For the owner

Things I assumed about Goan life that a Goan should check, separate from
the language questions above:

1. **Papad drying outside when the first rain comes.** I assumed a Goan
   household would have papad (or another sun-dried food — sango, fish,
   mango) out on a mat or rack when the first monsoon rain arrives without
   warning, giving Aaji something urgent and specific to call about,
   rather than a generic "come inside." If this isn't a common Goan
   monsoon moment, tell me what is and I'll rewrite around it.
2. **Laterite courtyard, bare feet.** I assumed an open laterite (jambha
   stone) courtyard or step that a child would cross barefoot, and that
   the stone visibly darkens with the first drops. If the house type this
   should evoke is different (a tiled verandah, a mud-and-dung-plastered
   yard, a concrete one), say so and I'll adjust the sensory detail.
3. **A frog answering the thunder near a well.** I used this as the first
   sound of the monsoon returning. If there's a more specifically Goan
   first-rain sound (a particular bird, the sound on a kaul-tiled roof,
   something else), I'd rather use the real one.
4. **Aaji as the grandmother's name/title, and a Marathi-speaking Goan
   family as the working choice.** I used "Aaji" (आजी), which fits a
   Marathi-speaking or Hindu Konkani Goan household, and it's why the
   Marathi retelling exists at all. For this spread, I'm treating the
   family as Marathi-speaking Goan, not Catholic Goan, as a working
   choice rather than a settled one. If it should be Catholic Goan
   instead, the household words change (grandmother, food, courtyard) and
   this Marathi text wouldn't apply in the same way. Worth deciding before
   Wave 7 grows this into a fuller book.
5. I did not use any Konkani, per the wave-4 brief (Sarvam has no Konkani
   voice; English and Marathi only for this proof of concept). "Aaji,"
   "kadai" and "papad" are used as loanwords inside the English text the
   way they'd appear in Goan English, not as Konkani.
